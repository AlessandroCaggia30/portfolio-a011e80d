Research Paper · Firm-Level Agent-Based Simulation

A Nash-Equilibrium Analysis of Incentive Contracts in Heterogeneous Firms

Version 2 · rebuilt with microfoundations · 30 seeds × 25 periods
Abstract. We simulate a firm with heterogeneous workers (ability ε, risk aversion ρ, effort curvature k, reciprocity φ) under nine incentive contracts A0–A8. Every effort choice is the Nash best response derived from a primary published source: Holmström–Milgrom (1987) for the piece rate, Holmström (1982) + Kandel–Lazear (1992) for team pay, Shapiro–Stiglitz (1984) for the efficiency wage, Lazear–Rosen (1981) for the tournament, MacLeod (2003) for the subjective bonus, and Holmström (1982) for career concerns. The three-tier hierarchy implements Tirole (1986) supervisor collusion with side-payment equilibrium. Parameters are calibrated to BBR (2005) fruit-picker moments; all reported statistics carry 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over 30 seeds. Results are compared against closed-form analytical Nash predictions, so the reader can locate the model's fit and its documented deviations.

1 · Introduction

The incentive-design literature has produced a large collection of analytical Nash-equilibrium predictions — Holmström (1979); Holmström & Milgrom (1987); Holmström (1982); Kandel & Lazear (1992); Shapiro & Stiglitz (1984); Lazear & Rosen (1981); MacLeod (2003); Tirole (1986). Each is proved under a stylised representative-agent assumption. What is missing (as Chang & Harrington 2006, Handbook of Computational Economics, explicitly note) is a simulator that (i) uses the actual best-response formulas from these papers, (ii) allows heterogeneity in the parameters they treat as fixed, and (iii) reports statistics with confidence intervals against the analytical benchmarks.

This paper builds such a simulator. Our four research questions:

  1. Q0 — Baseline. Does each contract's simulated effort match its analytical Nash prediction? Where does the population-of-heterogeneous-workers version deviate from the representative-agent formula?
  2. Q1 — Scale. How does group size N reshape the effort distribution and firm profit across contracts?
  3. Q2 — Rating noise. At what level of subjective-rating noise σm does A7 (MacLeod 2003) fail to sustain cooperation?
  4. Q3 — Collusion. Which contracts induce the most Tirole-1986 supervisor collusion? Which resist it?
  5. Q4 — ATE bias. If we treat 50% of workers with a productivity-augmenting technology, which contract structure produces the least biased naïve difference-in-means estimator?

Contribution. To our knowledge this is the first ABM to (a) enforce Nash-consistent best responses at every step, (b) carry uncertainty quantification on every reported statistic, and (c) systematically report the sim-vs-analytical percentage error as a validation criterion, following Fagiolo & Roventini (2017, JASSS) and Grazzini, Richiardi & Tsionas (2017, JEDC).

2 · Model

Agents. The firm has three tiers: workers (N heterogeneous individuals), supervisors (M ≈ N/5 middle managers), and a single principal (top management). Every worker draws attributes from a joint distribution — ability ε ~ 𝒩(1, 0.3), risk aversion ρ ~ 𝒩(2, 0.5), cost curvature k ~ 𝒩(1, 0.2), reciprocity φ ~ 𝒩(0.4, 0.15), discount δ ~ 𝒩(0.95, 0.02). Supervisors draw (skill θ, integrity ι, monitoring cost κ).

Per-period timing.

  1. Principal announces contract Aj and audit probability paudit.
  2. Every worker computes its Nash best-response effort ei* from the contract's microfoundation (§4 tabulates each formula).
  3. Signal realises: si = εi g(ei) + νi, ν ~ 𝒩(0, σ²).
  4. Supervisor observes si with skill-scaled noise σ / θj. If contract has β > 0, the supervisor solves a Tirole (1986) side-payment test and either colludes or reports honestly.
  5. Principal audits with probability paudit; detected collusion damages the supervisor's reputation.
  6. Wages paid; reference wages updated.

Best-response modifiers. Two adjustments follow the empirical literature: (i) reciprocity, e += φ (w − w̄) after Akerlof (1982) and Fehr & Falk (2002), and (ii) peer conformity, e += γ (ēN(i) − e), with γ = 0.30 following BBR (2005 §V). These are the only two mechanisms that deviate from pure Nash, and their coefficients come from empirical estimates — not free parameters.

7 · Q2 — Rating noise sensitivity

MacLeod (2003) predicts that subjective-bonus contracts (A7) sustain cooperation only when the rating noise σm is small enough that the worker trusts the supervisor's rating. We verify this by sweeping σm ∈ [0.1, 1.2] and observing the collapse.

8 · Q3 — Tirole (1986) supervisor collusion

Under contracts with β > 0 (piece rate, tournament, team pay), the worker has a positive rent from under-reporting productivity to the supervisor. Following Tirole (1986 Prop. 1) the supervisor accepts the side payment iff s ≥ ½(worker rent + supervisor reservation + paudit·penalty), and audits break collusion iff paudit·penalty ≥ worker rent. 10 · Discussion & limitations

What the simulation confirms. The Holmström (1982) free-rider prediction under A4 team pay reproduces at the qualitative level. The Kandel–Lazear (1992) peer-pressure attenuation matches direction (positive at small N, decays at large N). The Shapiro–Stiglitz (1984) efficiency-wage no-shirking condition supports high effort at our calibration. MacLeod's (2003) trust-and-noise mechanism produces the predicted A7 collapse in Figure 5. Tirole's (1986) collusion prediction under high-powered contracts is visible in Figure 6.

Where the simulation departs from pure Nash. The reciprocity + peer-conformity modifiers push effort above the analytical Nash under A1 (by roughly 30% at our calibration; see §5). This is deliberate — pure Nash + representative-agent produces predictions that fail empirically (Prendergast 1999, 2002). The deviation is documented, and the reader can inspect it in Figure 1.

Limitations.

  1. Single-shot Nash per period. We do not currently iterate best-response within a period to full fixed-point. This is a next-version item — Duffy (2006, HCE Vol. 2) shows fictitious-play convergence takes 5–50 iterations for games at this size.
  2. Static peer network. Following Gemkow–Neugart (2011), the network could evolve endogenously through referral / cooperation dynamics.
  3. No supervisor-effort choice. The current supervisor merely decides on collusion; a fuller Tirole (1986) game would let them choose monitoring intensity q.
  4. Symmetric contracts. All workers under the same contract; the paper's original hierarchy proposal (Rivkin–Siggelkow 2003 style) would let each tier face a different contract.
